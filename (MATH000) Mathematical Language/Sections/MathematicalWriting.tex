\section{Mathematical Writing}

\noindent\textbf{Mathematical Environments.}
\begin{ruletable}
Definition & Precise meaning of a term, object, property, or construction. \\
Notation & Introduction or declaration of mathematical notation. \\
Convention & Standing choice of notation, terminology, normalization, or usage for the stated context. \\
Axiom & Fundamental statement accepted without proof as part of a mathematical system. \\
Assumption & Premise adopted for the current statement, argument, construction, or discussion. \\
Hypothesis & Condition under which a result or theory is considered; traditionally named hypotheses retain their established terminology. \\
Conjecture & Statement proposed to be true but not proved in full generality. \\
Theorem & Principal mathematical result. \\
Proposition & Mathematical result of more limited scope or emphasis. \\
Lemma & Supporting result, usually used in another proof. \\
Corollary & Result following readily from a preceding result. \\
Claim & Local or intermediate assertion within a larger argument. \\
Fact & Established mathematical statement presented without the emphasis of a named result. \\
Observation & Mathematical feature or consequence readily recognized from the current context. \\
Proof & Rigorous argument establishing a mathematical assertion. \\
Sketch & Outline of the essential reasoning in a proof, construction, or solution, with some details omitted. \\
Formula & Distinguished mathematical identity or closed-form expression. \\
Algorithm & Explicit step-by-step procedure for carrying out a mathematical or computational task. \\
Construction & Specification of how a mathematical object is defined or built to meet stated requirements. \\
Design & Explanation of component roles and the rationale behind structural choices in a mathematical object, function, method, or environment. \\
Example & Concrete instance illustrating a definition, concept, method, or result. \\
Counterexample & Example disproving an assertion or showing that a condition is necessary. \\
Application & Use of a mathematical concept, method, or result in a specific mathematical or applied setting. \\
Recall & Reminder of previously introduced or assumed-known mathematical material relevant to the current discussion. \\
Motivation & Explanation of why a concept, method, problem, or development is introduced or worth considering. \\
Intuition & Informal insight suggesting what to expect or why a mathematical statement or method seems plausible, without constituting a proof. \\
Remark & Supplementary mathematical clarification, qualification, or extension of the surrounding material. \\
Note & Brief contextual or practical information, such as a usage instruction or permission. \\
Comment & Retrospective interpretation or takeaway concerning the preceding material. \\
Warning & Important notice of a subtlety, common error, or potentially misleading point. \\
Question & Question raised for thought, exploration, or discussion without necessarily requiring a complete solution. \\
Problem & Mathematical task intended to be solved. \\
Exercise & Task primarily intended to practice, apply, or reinforce mathematical material. \\
Open Problem & Problem not known to be solved in its stated form. \\
Hint & Partial clue intended to guide the reader toward a proof or solution without giving the complete argument or answer. \\
Suggestion & Optional recommendation to consider a particular observation, method, or approach. \\
Idea & Key insight guiding a proof, solution, or construction. \\
Strategy & Broad plan for approaching a problem, proof, or construction. \\
Tactic & Local step within a larger strategy. \\
Solution & Resolution of a stated problem or task by proof, computation, construction, or other appropriate means. \\
\end{ruletable}

Proof, sketch, and solution labels are written as \textit{Proof}, \textit{Sketch}, and \textit{Solution}, respectively, each followed by a period.

\begin{notationtable}
\ordinaryqed & Ending for ordinary proofs and solutions \\
\majorqed & Ending for substantial, difficult, or particularly important proofs \\
\exceptionalqed & Ending for exceptionally difficult, significant, or paper-level proofs \\
\end{notationtable}

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Enumeration and Subdivision.}
Enumeration labels are set in upright Roman type. Their form is determined by structural role rather than nesting depth.

\begin{ruletable}
\textup{1.1, 1.2, 1.3, \ldots} & Independent child problems or standalone units under a common parent; each is a distinct subproblem that may be stated, solved, and referenced separately. \\
\textup{(1), (2), (3), \ldots} & Ordinary parallel enumeration, such as choices, cases, possibilities, alternatives, examples, or procedural steps; not a subdivision of a single problem or a clause of a formal mathematical statement. \\
\textup{(a), (b), (c), \ldots} & Connected subparts of a single problem or task that share a common statement, setup, or context. \\
\textup{(i), (ii), (iii), \ldots} & Formal mathematical clauses within definitions, theorems, propositions, lemmas, and related statements, such as hypotheses, conditions, properties, conclusions, or equivalent statements. \\
\end{ruletable}

\noindent\textbf{Mathematical Shorthand.}\par
\vspace{0.10em}
Typeset abbreviations may be used in mathematical writing when appropriate; handwritten shorthand is reserved for personal notes, scratch work, and blackboard writing.
